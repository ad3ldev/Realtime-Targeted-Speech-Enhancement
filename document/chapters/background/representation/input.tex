\subsubsection{Time-Frequency (TF) Representation}
\begin{figure} [H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{images/audiorep/tf.png}
    \caption{A time-frequency representation. Time runs along one axis, frequency along the other. Each point's brightness reveals the sound's energy at that specific time and frequency.}
    \label{fig:tf}
\end{figure}
The TF representation can be thought of as a two-dimensional map, where one axis tracks time and the other maps frequency, like the different notes played. Each tiny square on the map shown in Figure \ref{fig:tf}, called a "TF bin," captures the energy (loudness) of the sound at that precise moment and frequency.

It can be visualized as a heatmap, where brighter colors usually hint at louder sounds, while darker shades whisper quieter ones. Some heatmaps even have a colorbar, like a legend, to clearly explain which colors correspond to which sound levels. So, by peering into this colorful landscape, we can learn how frequencies changes and shift over time, unlocking valuable insights into the inner workings of sound.

\subsubsection{Short-time Fourier Transform (STFT)}
\begin{figure} [H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{images/audiorep/stft.png}
    \caption{Computation process of short-time Fourier transform of waveform. Image by Bryan Pardo.}
    \label{fig:stft}
\end{figure}

The Short-time Fourier Transform (STFT) plays a crucial role in speech enhancement, providing a powerful lens through which we can dissect and manipulate audio signals.

To transform a continuous audio waveform into an STFT representation, we follow these steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dividing the Waveform:} The audio is first divided into a series of short, overlapping segments.
    \item \textbf{Applying the DFT:} For each segment, a mathematical operation called the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) extracts the individual frequencies present within that segment.
    \item \textbf{Constructing the Time-Frequency Matrix:} The STFT assembles these frequency snapshots into a two-dimensional matrix, creating a visual representation of how frequencies change over time.
\end{enumerate}
Each element within this matrix, called a TF bin, holds complex-valued information, encompassing both magnitude and phase:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Magnitude:} Represents the strength or energy of a particular frequency at a given time.
    \item \textbf{Phase:} Encodes the precise timing relationships between different frequencies.
\end{itemize}
The STFT is a reversible transformation, meaning we can reconstruct the original audio waveform from its STFT representation using a process called the inverse STFT (iSTFT).\\
In speech enhancement applications, the STFT's ability to decompose audio into its constituent frequencies over time proves invaluable for identifying and manipulating noise patterns, ultimately leading to clearer and more intelligible speech.\\
When computing STFT there exist multiple parameters to take into consideration here are some of them: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Window Types:} When analyzing audio for speech enhancement, the window type acts like a lens, shaping how frequencies are captured in short segments before the DFT. Different window shapes can highlight or downplay certain frequencies, influencing the final STFT representation. Figure \ref{fig:stft-windows} shows the most widely used windows when calculation STFT.
    \item \textbf{Window Length:} Window length dictates how many samples of audio are captured within each short segment before applying the DFT. This choice directly affects the frequency resolution of the resulting STFT representation; the longer the window the higher the frequency resolution and vice versa as shown in figure \ref{fig:window-len}.
    \item \textbf{Hop Length:} The hop length dictates the separation in samples, between successive short-time windows. Figure \ref{fig:hop-len} shows how adjusting the hop length can either extend or compress the time axis in a STFT.
    \item \textbf{Overlap-Add:} When converting a signal to an STFT and back to a waveform, certain parameter values for window type, window length, and hop length may not precisely recreate the original signal. A certain set of parameters, on the other hand, is theoretically guaranteed to properly reconstruct any signal. These parameter sets are known as Constant Overlap-Add (COLA) because they add up to a constant value when subsequent windows are applied.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Mel-spaced Spectrograms}
In terms of frequencies, human hearing is logarithmic! The Mel scale approximates this characteristic and is a simple technique to create a spectrogram's frequency axis. Because the number of Mel-spaced frequency bins is generally less than the number of linearly-spaced frequency bins, this is also commonly used to reduce the computational load on deep learning-based techniques.\\
Figure \ref{fig:specto} shows how the Mel-spectrogram compress higher frequencies and leave more space for lower frequencies.
