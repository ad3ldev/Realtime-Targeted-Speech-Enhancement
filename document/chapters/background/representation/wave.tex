Waveforms is the graphical representations of sound. Similar to ripples in a pond, sound waves are captured by a microphone and converted into an electrical signal. This signal undergoes segmentation (sampling) and quantization processes before materializing as the waveform visible on audio visualization platforms. The distinction between mono and stereo waveforms lies in the number of channels: a singular line for mono and dual lines for stereo.

Considering the recreation of an image by tracing outlines with dots, an analogy with audio recordings may be established to highlight the importance of sampling rate. The complexities of sample rate relate to the frequency of sound wave sampling each second, measured as Hertz (Hz). Higher sample rates allow for more detailed capture, particularly of high-pitched sounds. The Nyquist frequency, which represents half the sample rate, defines the highest limit for reliably recorded frequencies. Frequencies above the Nyquist frequency, sometimes known as the "sound ceiling," may cause distortion.

Additionally, downsampling, which involves reducing the sample rate to improve computational efficiency, is analogous to utilizing fewer dots in an image trace. While downsampling can speed up model training, careful consideration is required. An excessive reduction in sample rate risks deleting critical high-frequency components, thereby compromising the clarity and integrity of the enhanced speech output. As a result, choosing an optimal downsampling rate is critical for achieving a balance between computing efficiency and the preservation of critical audio data.
